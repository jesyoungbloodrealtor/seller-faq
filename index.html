17 Questions to Ask Before You Sell Your House

Straight answers on price, timing, repairs and your next move, backed by the latest numbers.

Jes YoungbloodRegal Realtors
Published November 2026

In October, Google searches for “should I sell my house” hit a record high.

“Should I sell?” is the question everyone asks first. It’s also the hardest one to answer first.

You can’t answer it until you know what you’d walk away with, what waiting costs, and what happens if your house sits.

Those are the 17 questions below. Each one is answered with the latest numbers.

Part 1

Afraid of leaving money on the table?

Most pricing mistakes aren’t the ones sellers worry about.

01What if my house doesn’t sell?

It’s one of the most common worries, and you’re not alone. Searches for “can’t sell house” also hit a record high this spring. They’ve stayed near that high all fall.

Source: Google Trends, U.S. searches for “can’t sell house,” April to October 2026

Part of it is the market. In October, there were 1.59 million homes for sale across the country, up 4.6% from a year earlier. But sales were down 8.8%. Buyers have more choices, so they take their time. Your house isn’t only competing for a buyer’s budget. It’s competing with the other listings.

But most homes that don’t sell have the same three problems: the price, how the home looks in photos and in person, and how many of the right buyers see it. You decide all three before the sign goes up. Get them right at the start, and your home is much less likely to sit.

02Is it a mistake to start high and come down later?

Usually, yes. Buyers compare your home with everything else in their price range. If yours is priced too high, the other homes look like better deals. By the time you lower the price, the home has been for sale for a while. Buyers start to wonder what’s wrong with it.

It’s happening a lot this fall. In mid-October, 22% of homes for sale had a price cut. That’s the most on record.

Source: Redfin, four weeks ending October 11, 2026

Homes that start too high often sell for less in the end.

03How much will I really walk away with?

Start with the price your home will likely sell for, not the list price. Then subtract:

  • What you still owe on your mortgage
  • Agent fees and closing costs
  • Any money you give the buyer for repairs or costs
  • Moving costs

That third one happens more than most sellers think. In August, sellers gave buyers money toward repairs or costs in nearly 45% of sales. That’s the most for any August since at least 2020. Before you list, I can write down what you’d walk away with at a few different prices. Then the number at closing won’t surprise you.

Source: Redfin, August 2026

04Will I owe capital gains tax when I sell?

Most people selling the home they live in won’t owe it. But some people do. They have often owned their home a long time in a place where prices went up a lot.

Right now, you can make up to $250,000 in profit tax-free if you’re single, or $500,000 if you’re married and file taxes together. In most cases, you must have owned and lived in the home for two of the last five years.

Those limits were set in 1997, when the typical U.S. home sold for about $145,000. This spring, it was about $410,000. The limits haven’t moved. About a third of homeowners, 29 million people, could already have more profit than the $250,000 single limit.

Sources: U.S. Census Bureau and HUD via FRED, median sales price of houses sold, 1997 and Q2 2026; National Association of REALTORS®, The Capital Gains Cliff Is Coming

Some lawmakers want to raise or remove these limits, but no change has passed. If you’ve owned your home a long time, talk to a tax expert before you list, not after.

05What’s my house actually worth?

The best answer comes from what homes like yours, nearby, sold for in the last few months. Then you adjust for what makes your home different: condition, updates, layout, lot size, even the street.

Many sellers start with an online estimate. That’s a fine place to begin. But those tools use public records and big trends. They can’t see the kitchen you redid, the roof you replaced, or the busy road two houses down. And the sales they use can be months old.

That’s why I make careful home value reports for my clients. Each one uses the newest sales in our market and the local details online tools miss. I walk you through it, so you can see exactly how we got to the number.

Part 2

You’re wondering if now is the right time.

Here’s what the calendar can and can’t tell you.

06Should I sell now, or wait?

The honest answer: sell when it fits your life and your money, not when the market looks perfect. You probably know this, but no one can predict the market. And waiting costs money too. Taxes, insurance and upkeep cost the typical homeowner about $1,380 a month. That’s about $45 a day.

A better question: will your reasons for moving still be true in six months? If so, waiting just puts off your move.

07Will I lose money if I sell in the winter?

Not necessarily. Over the last decade, winter sellers did better than summer and fall sellers. ATTOM studied ten years of home sales. Homes that sold in December, January and February got more over their expected value than homes that sold from July through November.

Spring is still usually the best season. But there are fewer homes for sale in winter, so you have less competition.

08How long will my house take to sell?

A lot of people are asking. Searches for “how long does it take to sell a house” hit a record high in September.

Source: Google Trends, U.S. searches for “how long does it take to sell a house,” September 2026

It depends on your price and your neighborhood. The best guide is how fast homes like yours are selling here.

What matters most is the first couple of weeks. That’s when a new listing gets the most attention. If buyers aren’t showing interest early, something needs to change.

09Once I accept an offer, how long until it’s done?

Usually several weeks. The buyer’s loan sets most of the schedule. Cash sales can close faster. During that time, there’s an inspection, an appraisal (the lender’s check on the home’s value), loan approval and paperwork on the home’s ownership.

You can help keep things on time. Have your disclosure forms, permits and repair records ready before you list. Then answer requests quickly.

Part 3

Before you spend a dollar on repairs

Some fixes pay you back. Most don’t.

10Should I fix it up, or sell it as is?11Which repairs are a waste of money?12Is staging worth the money?

10Should I fix it up, or sell it as is?

Usually, somewhere in between.

Selling as is saves time and money now. But buyers guess what the work will cost, often too high, and take that off their offer. A full remodel is a bet. You pay now and hope buyers love it as much as you do.

The middle path is fixing what buyers will notice in the photos, on a tour, and in the inspection report.

Lenders say the house kills more deals than the loan does. In a 2026 survey, they said problems with the home ruin more deals than loan problems. An inspection before you list tells you what an inspector will find before a buyer does.

And selling as is doesn’t change what you have to tell buyers. In most states, you must share any problems you know about. It protects everyone.

11Which repairs are a waste of money?

Big projects buyers will likely redo in their own style, like a full kitchen or bathroom remodel. Those rarely pay you back. The same goes for replacing older lights, faucets and appliances that still work.

Spend your money on safety, things that work, and first impressions instead. Fix leaks, broken systems, worn floors in main rooms, and anything an inspector will likely point out. And whatever you don’t fix, tell buyers what you know about it.

12Is staging worth the money?

It depends on the room. The basics cost very little: clear the clutter, clean, put away family photos, and let in as much light as you can. Full staging means bringing in furniture and decor. It helps most in empty or very old-looking rooms, where buyers have a hard time picturing the space. It matters most in photos, because that’s where most buyers see your home first.

Part 4

Sell, rent, or stay put?

Each one has a cost. Here’s how to weigh them.

13What if it sells and I have nowhere to go?14Do I have to sell before I can buy?15Should I rent it out instead of selling?16Is it worth giving up my low mortgage rate?

13What if it sells and I have nowhere to go?

This worry stops many people from selling at all. In a 2026 HomeLight survey, it was the second-biggest fear sellers have. You have more options than you might think:

  • A closing date that gives you time to move
  • A rent-back, where you stay in the home for a set time after it sells
  • An offer on your next home that only goes through if yours sells
  • Loans or programs that let you buy before you sell

Source: HomeLight, agent survey, December 2025

The right one depends on your money and our local market. The key is to decide the order before you list.

14Do I have to sell before I can buy?

Not always. You have a few choices:

  • Make your offer on the next home depend on yours selling
  • Sell first, then rent your home back for a while
  • Use a loan that lets you buy first

Each one moves the risk around. None of them removes it. So talk to a lender early. Know what you can afford, and what you’d do if the timing slips.

15Should I rent it out instead of selling?

More homeowners are asking. Searches for “rent or sell house” hit a record high in September.

Renting can make sense if your mortgage rate is low, the rent covers your costs, and you’re ready to be a landlord. That means repairs, empty months, tenant laws, insurance, and different tax rules. Renting out a home that didn’t sell is not the same as choosing to invest. Do the math both ways before you decide.

16Is it worth giving up my low mortgage rate?

It’s a real cost. If your rate is much lower than today’s, your next loan will likely cost more each month, even on a similar home.

But your rate is only part of the decision. Staying has costs too: a long commute, a home you’ve outgrown, living far from family. Those don’t show up on a bill. Compare the extra monthly cost with what staying costs you in other ways.
